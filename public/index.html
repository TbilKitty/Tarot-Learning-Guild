Learn to read tarot,
one connection at a time.

Reading tarot is an interpretive practice. A card has a theme; the question, its position, and its neighbors help you decide which part of that theme deserves attention. Use this guide to practice making a careful interpretation instead of memorizing a single prediction for each card.

01

Ask an open question

“What should I consider about this relationship?” gives you more to work with than “Will they call on Friday?” An open question directs your attention to patterns and choices while leaving room to check facts outside the reading.

02

Read the position

Here, card one shows the situation, card two what shapes it, card three a challenge, card four a possible next step, and card five a direction to consider. “Possible direction” is an invitation to reflect, not a promise of a future event.

03

Compare the cards

Look for repeated themes and useful tension. A card about action next to a card about rest could ask what must be settled before moving. Your question determines which reading of those themes is most useful.

What do the suits suggest?

Wands often point toward motivation, initiative, and creative effort. Cups invite attention to emotion and connection. Swords raise questions about thought, communication, and conflict. Pentacles bring material conditions, work, health, and resources into view. The 22 Major Arcana cards can bring broader patterns and turning points into focus. Suit themes are starting points; individual cards and positions can change the emphasis.

How should I read a reversal?

A reversed card does not always mean the opposite of its upright meaning. Try three possibilities: Is the theme blocked? Is it unfolding privately or inwardly? Is the upright lesson overdue or excessive? For example, Strength reversed might invite attention to insecurity or depleted patience, rather than a claim that you have no strength. If reversals distract you, turn them off while learning the core meanings.

When do three clarifiers help?

Use the three clarifiers after reading all five main cards. Treat them as one shared lens on the complete spread: notice what they reinforce, complicate, or redirect across the story. They add detail to the original question; they are not three new predictions or separate clarifiers for each card.

Try a complete example

Imagine Two of Cups in the situation position and Eight of Swords in the challenge position. Connection may matter, while assumptions or a sense of being stuck complicate it. If Page of Swords, Four of Cups, and Justice clarify the whole spread, you might ask what questions could test an assumption, where attention has withdrawn, and what a fair account of the overall situation would include. None of those cards can tell you what another person privately thinks.
